A phone camera is more than good enough for grading. What limits the result is
never the sensor; it is light, framing, and three habits phones encourage.

## The three phone habits that ruin a grading photo

**Shooting at an angle.** Phones are held, so it is natural to lean over a
garment at forty-five degrees. That foreshortens one end, distorts the shape and
makes a straight hem look uneven. Get the phone parallel to the garment and shoot
straight down.

**Casting a shadow.** You are standing over the thing you are photographing,
between it and the light. Move so the light comes past you rather than through
you, or step to the side and hold the phone out.

**Portrait mode.** It blurs the background deliberately, and it softens the
garment's edges as a side effect. Those edges are part of what structural
condition is read from. Use the normal camera.

## The setup

Flat on a plain surface. Bare floor, plain table, a smoothed single-colour
sheet. No patterned duvet, no rug, nothing that puts texture in the frame that
is not on the garment.

Near a window, on an overcast day. Cloud is a diffuser the size of the sky, and
it produces exactly the even light a lightbox is engineered to fake.

Smooth the fabric out. Every fold is a strip the assessment cannot see, and it
will be appropriately cautious about the part it cannot see.

## Let the app prompt you

The capture flow asks for the required set in order: front, back, care label, and
at least one detail. Following the prompts is faster than remembering, and it is
how you avoid getting home and finding you never photographed the label.

Take the detail shots you were going to skip. Every close-up turns an inference
into an observation, and photographing a flaw costs you a fraction of a point
while hiding one costs you the buyer.

## Locking exposure

Most phone cameras will let you tap and hold to lock exposure and focus. Do it
on the garment, not on the bright background.

Without it, a dark garment on a light floor gets exposed for the floor, and the
garment ends up too dark to read texture from. Texture is what the heaviest
factor is scored from.

## Shooting a set at once

If you have twenty things to do, do them as a session with the light set up once
rather than one at a time whenever you think of it.

Lay out, shoot the four, move it aside, next. Twenty garments takes about half an
hour that way and considerably longer any other way.

## What not to do afterwards

Do not edit them. No filters, no brightness boost, no colour correction.

Adjusting a grading photo makes the assessment less accurate, which is the
opposite of what you want. Save the editing for the listing photos, which are a
different set with a different job. See
[Photographing for a listing](/help/flipdesk/photographing-for-a-listing).

## Cleaning the lens

The single cheapest improvement available, and the one nobody does.

A phone lives in a pocket. The lens picks up a film that softens every photo
slightly, and softness is exactly what costs you in a fabric assessment, because
pilling and weave are small-detail judgements.

Wipe it on something clean before a session. It takes two seconds and it is worth
more than any setting on the camera.

## Volume buttons and stability

Pressing the on-screen shutter moves the phone. Using a volume button as the
shutter moves it less, and bracing your elbows on the table moves it least.

At the distances involved, a small amount of shake is enough to soften the detail
you are trying to capture.

## If the room is genuinely dark

One light, bounced off a white wall or ceiling, rather than pointed at the
garment. Bounced light is soft; direct light is hard and produces the shadows
that read as marks.

Never the flash. On-camera flash flattens texture completely, and texture is what
the heaviest scoring factor is read from. A slightly dark photo taken in bounced
light beats a bright one taken with flash every time.
