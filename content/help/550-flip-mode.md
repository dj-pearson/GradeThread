Condition Check answers the buyer's question about a listing. Flip mode answers
the seller's: should I buy this and resell it.

It needs a FlipDesk account, and it runs only when you ask.

## What it tells you

On a listing detail page, it takes the listing's own photos and works out:

**A resale range**, from condition-matched sold comps rather than from active
listings.

**Margin after fees**, meaning what you would actually keep, not the difference
between the two prices.

**Break-even**, the price above which buying it stops making sense.

**Days to sell**, an estimate from how quickly similar items in similar condition
have moved.

**Buy or pass**, which is the summary of the above and is a suggestion rather
than an instruction.

## It costs something to ask

This is the one mode that is always click-to-run and will not gain an automatic
setting.

The buyer's condition check has a free tier because it is a read of something you
are already looking at. Flip mode shadow-grades the item and pulls comps, which
spends a metered AI action against your plan.

A feature that spends things should never fire because you scrolled past
something. So it is a button, every time, and the button says what it is about
to do.

## The shadow grade stays private

To produce a resale range it has to assess the condition, and that assessment is
a grade in everything but status.

It is private to your workspace and is **never written as a grade record**. It
produces no certificate, appears on no public page, and is attached to nobody's
seller profile.

The reason is straightforward: a certificate is a claim made from photos shot
for grading, at the required angles, with the label legible. A read of somebody
else's styled listing photos is a useful private estimate and is not that. Letting
it become a certificate would put a weaker assessment behind the same badge as a
proper one, which would devalue every certificate that was done properly.

## Reading it honestly

Two cautions worth holding.

**Confidence is usually lower** than a proper grade, because listing photos are
styled rather than flat and the care label is rarely photographed. A wide resale
range often reflects an uncertain condition read rather than an uncertain market.

**The comps are the market, not a promise.** Days to sell is an average over
items that did sell. Items that did not sell are, by construction, not in the
data.

## Where it fits

The natural use is sourcing from another marketplace: somebody is selling
something under-described, you can see it is better than they said, and the
question is whether the spread survives fees and postage.

That is exactly the question the panel answers, and it answers it in about the
time it takes to read the listing title.

## Comparing it to a proper appraisal

Flip mode is the fast version of a question FlipDesk answers more thoroughly once
the item is in your inventory.

Once you own the garment and have photographed it properly, a real grade and real
comps give you a firmer number, because the assessment is made from photos shot
for the purpose rather than from somebody's styled listing pictures.

The point of flip mode is that it happens before you buy, when the fast, less
certain answer is the only one available and is still enormously better than a
guess.

## What to do with a marginal verdict

Most sourcing decisions are not obvious, which is why a range is more honest than
a single number.

When the margin is thin, the deciding factors are usually days to sell and how
much of your attention the item will take. A slim margin on something that moves
in a week is a business. The same margin on something that sits for four months
is a shelf.

The panel gives you both numbers for exactly that reason.
