The grade can only assess what the photos show. That sentence explains almost
every surprising grade anyone has ever received, so it is worth taking
seriously.

Four photos are required. Getting them right takes about two minutes and is the
difference between a confident grade and one that goes to review.

## Front

The whole garment, flat, filling the frame, shot from directly above.

Not on a hanger. Not at an angle. Not from across the room with the garment
occupying a third of the picture. The model reads shape, drape and evenness of
wear from this shot, and all three are distorted by a hanger and lost at a
distance.

Smooth it out first. A fold across the chest hides whatever is under the fold,
and the grade will be cautious about the part it cannot see.

## Back

Same again, turned over.

This is the shot people skip and it is where wear actually lives. Collar
grubbiness, seat shine on trousers, shoulder fade from a coat hook, the worn
patch where a rucksack sat. A front-only submission systematically over-grades,
which sounds good until the buyer turns it over.

## Care label

Close enough to read the text.

Two things come off it: fibre composition and size. Composition changes what
counts as wear, because pilling on lambswool at eighteen months is normal and
the same pilling on an acrylic blend is not. Feeding the model a blurry label
costs you accuracy in fabric condition, which is the heaviest factor at 30
percent.

If the label is gone or illegible, say so in the item fields rather than
leaving it blank.

## At least one detail

A cuff, a hem, an armpit, a zip pull, a knee. If the garment has a flaw,
photograph the flaw close up.

More detail shots help. This is the one place where adding photos genuinely
improves the result, because each one turns an inference into an observation.

More full-length shots do not help. The second and third front photo tell the
model nothing the first did not.

## Why showing the flaw helps you

This is the counterintuitive part, and it is the single most useful thing in
this article.

A photographed flaw is scored. A snag on a sleeve costs a fraction of a point
in fabric condition and the report names it, which means your listing can name
it too.

An unphotographed flaw is not scored, because nothing can score what it cannot
see. What it does instead is lower the model's confidence, and a grade below the
confidence threshold is held for a human reviewer rather than published. So
hiding the flaw does not buy you a higher grade; it buys you a delay.

And then the buyer receives the garment, finds the snag, and opens a
not-as-described case. That costs the return shipping, the item, and the
account metric. The snag was worth 0.2.

## The setting

Flat, plain, even light. In practice: a bare floor or a plain table, near a
window, on an overcast day.

Direct sunlight makes hard shadows that read as marks. Warm indoor bulbs shift
the colour enough that a white shirt grades as cream and a black one loses its
depth. A patterned duvet puts texture in the frame that is not on the garment.

[Lighting and background](/help/grading/lighting-and-background) goes into this
properly, including what to do when the only light you have is a kitchen at
nine at night.

## Before you submit

Check three things:

1. Can you read the care label at full zoom?
2. Is the back photo actually the back, not the front again?
3. Is every flaw you would mention in the listing visible in a photo?

If all three are yes, the grade will be confident and it will match what you
would have said yourself. That agreement is the point.
