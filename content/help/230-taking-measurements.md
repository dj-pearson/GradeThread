Sizing is the largest single cause of "not as described" returns in clothing,
and it is almost entirely preventable. A label size is a manufacturer's opinion
that has drifted for thirty years. A measurement is a fact.

## Measure flat, and say so

Lay the garment flat, smooth it, and measure across, not around.

Then label it as such. "Chest 21 inches flat" is unambiguous. "Chest 21 inches"
next to a garment somebody assumes is a circumference is a buyer ordering
something half their size.

This is the convention across resale and it is worth following even though
doubling is trivial, because consistency between listings is what lets a repeat
buyer trust your numbers.

## What to take, by garment

**Tops and shirts.** Pit to pit, length from the highest shoulder point, sleeve
from shoulder seam to cuff. Shoulder width across the back if it is structured.

**Trousers and jeans.** Waist flat, inseam, rise, leg opening. Inseam and rise
are the two that decide whether they fit; waist alone is not enough.

**Dresses.** Bust, waist, hip, total length. Length matters more than people
expect, because it is what makes a dress read as a midi or a maxi on a specific
person.

**Outerwear.** Everything from tops, plus the shoulder width, because a coat
that does not sit right on the shoulders cannot be fixed by anything else.

**Skirts.** Waist flat, length, hip if it is fitted.

The measurement fields you see depend on the category you catalogued, which is
one of the reasons to get the category right first.

## Be consistent about where you measure from

The number matters less than measuring the same way every time.

Length from the highest shoulder point, not from the collar seam. Sleeve from
the shoulder seam, not from the neck. Waist at the narrowest, not wherever the
tape landed.

Pick your convention, use it on everything, and a buyer who bought from you once
can order the next thing without asking a question. That repeat buyer is worth
considerably more than the four minutes.

## The tape

A soft tailor's tape, not a builder's rule. Keep it flat on the garment and do
not pull it tight; pulling adds an inch to knitwear and none to a woven, which
means your numbers stop being comparable to each other.

Round to the nearest half inch or half centimetre. Precision past that is false,
and a buyer treating 20.75 as exact is a buyer you have misled.

## Why it is worth the time

A return costs you the outbound postage, the return postage, the fee that may
not come back, the time handling it, and a hit to your metrics on the channel.
Against that, four minutes with a tape.

It is also the fastest thing to do at volume, because measuring twenty items in
one session with the tape already out is a different job from measuring one item
whenever you happen to think of it.

## Skipping it

Perfectly reasonable on anything unfitted or cheap. A tote bag, a scarf, a
beanie: the measurement adds nothing a photo does not already say.

The rule of thumb is fit risk. If somebody could receive it and find it does not
fit, measure it. If they could not, do not bother.

## Recording them once

Measurements attach to the item, so they carry into the listing on every channel
without being retyped, and into the description in whichever format you have set.

That is the argument for entering them in FlipDesk rather than typing them into
a listing directly. Typed into one listing, they exist on one channel and are
gone when you relist. Recorded on the item, they survive relisting, cross-posting
and the item coming back as a return.
