A certificate is a public page showing a garment's condition assessment. If a
seller has one, checking it takes about ten seconds and is the single most
useful thing you can do before buying pre-owned clothing online.

## Three ways to check

**Follow the link.** Sellers put it in the listing description. Click it.

**Scan the QR code.** Printed on tags and shown on the certificate itself. Point
a phone camera at it.

**Type the number.** Go to [the verify page](/verify) and enter the certificate
number. This is the route to use when somebody has given you a number rather
than a link.

## What the page shows

**The grade.** A number between 1.0 and 10.0, and a tier name.

**The five factors**, each scored, each with its published weight. This is the
part worth reading, because it tells you where the wear is. A 7.0 that lost its
points on fabric and a 7.0 that lost them on a broken zip are different
purchases, and only one of them is fixable.

**The photos it was assessed from.** You can see what the assessment saw.

**When it was graded**, and whether a human reviewer checked it.

**How it was produced.** Every certificate states plainly that the assessment is
automated, from the seller's photos, and whether a person reviewed it before it
published.

## Why a screenshot is not enough

A screenshot is an image of a claim rather than the claim itself. It can be
edited, it can belong to a different garment, and it cannot be checked.

If a seller offers a screenshot, ask for the link or the number. A seller with a
real certificate can produce either in seconds. One who cannot is telling you
something.

## When it does not resolve

**Not found.** No certificate with that number. Either it was mistyped, or it
does not exist.

**Withheld.** The certificate exists but is not currently public. This happens
while something is under moderation or review. It is not a verdict, and it is
also not a green light: treat the listing as ungraded until it resolves.

**Revised.** The grade was changed after it was issued, by a dispute or a
regrade. The page shows the current values and says plainly that it was revised
rather than quietly swapping the number, so if you screenshotted an earlier
version you can see that it changed rather than concluding you misread it.

## What a certificate does not tell you

**Whether the item is genuine.** Condition grading is not authentication. A
garment can be a perfect 9.5 and be a fake.

**What it is worth.** Condition is one lever on price. Brand, size, season and
demand move it more.

**Anything that was not photographed.** The assessment covers what the photos
showed. That is why the photos are on the page: you can judge for yourself
whether the coverage was thorough.

## If the item arrives wrong

If a graded item turns up materially different from its certificate, that is
what the [buyer guarantee](/help/buyers/the-buyer-guarantee) exists for, and it
has a claim process of its own.

You can also report a certificate directly from its page, which routes to
moderation rather than to the seller.

## What to do with the factor breakdown

The overall number decides whether to keep reading. The five factors decide
whether to buy.

A 7.2 that lost its points on **fabric** has lost them permanently. Pilling,
thinning and fading are the garment's remaining life, and no buyer fixes them.

A 7.2 that lost its points on **functional elements** has a broken zip or a
missing button. That is a twenty-minute repair and a bargaining position.

A 7.2 held back by **odour and cleanliness** is often a wash away, and that
factor carries the least weight anyway, so the underlying garment is better than
the number suggests.

Two identical scores, three completely different purchases. That distinction is
the thing a certificate gives you that a seller's adjective never can, and it is
free to read.

## Asking the seller

If the photos on the certificate do not cover something you care about, ask.

A seller with a certificate has already demonstrated they will photograph a
garment properly, so a request for one more angle is usually answered quickly. A
seller who will not is telling you something for free.
