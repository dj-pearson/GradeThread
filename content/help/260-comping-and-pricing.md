Comping is looking at what similar items actually sold for, so your price is
evidence rather than a guess. It is optional, and it is the difference between
a considered price and a hopeful one.

## Sold, not active

The single most important distinction.

An **active listing** is a price somebody is asking. Nobody has agreed to it.
Twenty active listings at 60 pounds with none selling is evidence that 60 pounds
is too much, which is the opposite of what it looks like.

A **sold listing** is a price somebody paid. That is a fact about the market.

FlipDesk pulls sold comps by default for exactly this reason. When you look at
actives, look at them to see who you are competing against, not to set your
price.

## What makes a comp comparable

Brand, category and size, first. A comp for a different size of the same jacket
is only loosely a comp, because size is one of the strongest demand signals in
clothing.

Then condition. This is where the grade earns its keep: a 9.0 and a 6.0 of the
same garment are genuinely different products, and averaging their sold prices
gives you a number that describes neither. Condition-matched comps are the ones
worth reading.

Then recency. Clothing is seasonal and fashion moves. Three months is a
reasonable window for most things and too long for anything trend-driven.

## Reading the spread

You will get a range rather than a number, and the range is the useful part.

A **tight** spread means the market agrees, and you should price inside it. Going
above is how an item sits for four months.

A **wide** spread usually means the comps are not really comparable: different
condition, different size, or one outlier that was a mispriced steal. Narrow the
search before trusting the average.

The suggested price is built from the condition-matched middle of that spread,
not the top of it. You can override it, and the honest question when you do is
whether you have information the comps do not, or whether you just want more
money.

## When there are no comps

It happens with genuinely unusual items, and there are three moves.

**Widen the brand.** Comp against a similar brand at a similar market position
rather than an identical label.

**Comp the category.** A plain merino crew neck in a good size sells for
something fairly predictable regardless of who made it.

**Price to test.** List high, watch the watchers, and drop in steps. This is
slower and it is the only honest option for something with no market history.

## Condition and price

Condition moves price smoothly, not in seven steps, which is why the number
matters more than the tier.

The [Condition Index](/condition-index) publishes the shape of that curve from
real sold data, and it is worth looking at once for the categories you sell,
because the slope is steeper than most people expect at the top end. The
difference between an 8 and a 9 is often larger than between a 6 and a 7.

## Then the price sticks

Once you set it, the item moves to Comped and the price carries into the
composer, into every channel you cross-post to, and into the repricing rules if
you use them.

Changing it later in one place changes it in the draft. Changing it after a
listing is live is a revision on that channel, which is a different operation
and is covered in
[Publishing a listing](/help/flipdesk/publishing-a-listing).

## Price is a decision you can revisit

Nothing about setting a price is permanent. Repricing rules can step a price
down over time, and a manual edit is always available.

What is expensive is a price set high enough that nobody watches the listing at
all. A price slightly too high collects watchers you can send an offer to. A
price far too high collects nothing, and there is no signal to act on later.

Starting near the comped range and stepping down beats starting hopeful.
