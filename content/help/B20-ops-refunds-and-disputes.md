Internal. The money mechanics are in [[billing-refunds]] in the vault; this is
about telling the three cases apart, because they arrive worded identically.

## Three different things

**A billing refund.** Somebody was charged and wants the money back. Wrong
purchase, double charge, charged after cancelling.

**A grading dispute.** A seller thinks a grade is wrong. No money involved. It
routes to a reviewer and either confirms or revises the grade.

**A buyer guarantee claim.** A buyer received a graded item materially different
from its certificate. This one has money and a grading question in it, and they
are decided separately.

Nearly every misrouted ticket is one of these treated as another. A seller
saying "the grade is wrong, I want a refund" is usually a dispute wearing a
refund's clothes, and refunding it settles nothing.

## The statutory floor

For consumers in the EU and the UK, statutory rights sit above the published
[refund policy](/refund) and the policy cannot reduce them.

That is not a courtesy. A policy term contradicting a statutory right is
unenforceable rather than binding, so "the policy says thirty days" is not an
answer to somebody exercising a statutory right.

When a customer invokes one, escalate rather than quoting the policy back.

## Grading disputes are evidence

A dispute that finds a grade materially wrong is not only a customer outcome. It
feeds the accuracy measurement published on
[the transparency report](/transparency).

That is why disputes are worked properly even when the commercial stakes are
small, and why "confirm it to close the ticket" is the one wrong way to resolve
one. The published error rate is only worth anything because the confirmations
and reversals behind it are real.

## Guarantee claims

Decided by comparing the certificate's photographs against the buyer's
photographs of what arrived.

The buyer's photographs are the constraint. A claim raised after the garment has
been washed or worn is a claim about a different garment, which is why the public
article tells buyers to photograph before doing anything else.

The three qualifying situations are published on
[the guarantee page](/buyer-guarantee) and are the definition, not a summary of
one.

## What never happens

**No refund is issued to make a grading dispute go away.** They are separate
questions and conflating them corrupts the accuracy data.

**No grade is revised without a reviewer looking.** A revision that was really a
customer-service decision is a false entry in a public record.

## Routing quickly

The question that sorts almost every ticket: what does this person want changed?

**A charge** goes to billing. **A grade** goes to review. **A garment they
received** goes to the guarantee.

Somebody wanting two of those wants two tickets, or one ticket worked on two
tracks. Merging them into a single decision is how one gets quietly dropped.

## Tone

A person disputing a grade believes they know their own garment better than a
model does, and they are often right.

Treating a dispute as a complaint to be managed produces worse data and worse
customers. It is a measurement request, and the honest answer when a reviewer
confirms the original is a short explanation of what the photographs showed, not
a policy quote.

## Keeping the record straight

Whatever the outcome, the reason belongs on the record.

A dispute confirmed at the original grade should say what the photographs
showed. A revision should say which factor moved and why. A refund should say
what it was for.

None of that is bureaucracy: it is what makes a second contact from the same
person answerable by reading rather than by re-litigating, and it is what makes
the accuracy figures traceable to real decisions.
