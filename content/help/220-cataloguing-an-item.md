Cataloguing is where an item stops being "a jacket" and becomes a specific
thing. It is a five-minute job that every later stage depends on.

## The fields

**Brand.** The single most valuable field on the screen. It drives the comps
that set your price, it fills the eBay item specific that most affects search
placement, and buyers filter by it. Get it right, including the exact spelling
the marketplace uses.

**Category.** What kind of garment it is. This decides which measurement fields
appear, which item specifics the marketplace will demand, and which comps are
relevant. It is the field to get right first, because it changes the shape of
everything after it.

**Size.** As printed on the label, plus the system it belongs to. A UK 12 and a
US 12 are different garments and a buyer who receives the wrong one returns it.

**Colour.** Use the word a buyer would search, not the one on the swatch card.
"Navy" finds buyers. "Midnight" does not.

**Condition notes.** Anything you can see that the photos might not carry on
their own. This is separate from the grade and it is where the specifics live.

**SKU.** Optional, and worth adding above about fifty items. It is what ties a
thing on a shelf to a row on a screen.

## Why category first

Changing the category later is supported and it is not free.

The measurement set changes, because a dress and a pair of trousers are measured
differently. The item specifics change, because eBay asks different required
questions per category. Some of what you already entered has an equivalent in
the new category and some does not.

What happens is a value-preserving remap: fields with an equivalent carry over,
fields without one are cleared, and you are shown what changed before it is
applied rather than discovering it in a published listing.

That is a good safety net and it is still slower than picking correctly the
first time.

## Working in bulk

Cataloguing one item at a time is the slow way. The prep view in Inventory shows
many items at once with the same fields on each, which suits a session where you
unpack twenty things and catalogue them in one pass.

Select several items and set a field across all of them. Everything from one lot
usually shares a source, often a category, and sometimes a brand.

## What the AI fills in

If you added the item from a photo, several fields arrive pre-filled: likely
brand, category, colour, size if a label was visible.

Treat those as a draft. They are usually right and they are occasionally
confidently wrong, and a confidently wrong brand propagates into comps, price
and item specifics without anything looking broken. Two seconds of checking each
one is the correct amount of scepticism.

## What good cataloguing buys you

Three things you feel later.

Comps that are actually comparable, because the search is built from brand,
category and size rather than from your title.

Item specifics that fill themselves, because eBay's required fields map from
what you already entered instead of being typed again per listing.

Findability. Two hundred items in, Search is how you locate anything, and it
searches what you catalogued.

## Getting the brand spelling right

Use the spelling the marketplace uses, not the one on the label.

Brands that trade under a shortened name, brands with punctuation, and brands
that changed their styling at some point are all cases where the label and the
marketplace disagree. eBay's item specific expects its own value, and a
near-miss is treated as a different brand entirely, which means your listing
misses the filter.

When the composer offers a brand suggestion, it is drawing on the marketplace's
own list. That is the one to take.
