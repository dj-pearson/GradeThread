Some garments carry a passport: a printed tag or a code that resolves to the
item's history rather than to a single assessment. Scanning one takes a couple of
seconds and works without an account.

## How to scan

**Point your camera at the QR code** on the tag. Most phones open it without an
app.

**Or type the code** at [the scan page](/scan) if the QR will not read, which
happens with worn or creased tags.

Either way you land on a public page. No sign-in, no app, nothing to install.
That was a hard requirement: a verification that needs an account is a
verification nobody performs standing at a market stall.

## What a passport shows

**Every grade the garment has received**, with dates. This is the part a single
certificate cannot give you.

**Its listing history**, where it has been offered.

**Its ownership record**, where the previous owners chose to be recorded.

## Why a history beats a snapshot

One assessment tells you the condition on one date. Two assessments five years
apart tell you how the garment has held up, which is a different and often more
useful fact.

A coat graded 9.0 when new and 8.4 after four years has lost very little. A
different coat graded 9.0 and then 6.2 over the same period has told you
something about how that fabric wears that no single number could.

For anything you expect to keep, that trajectory is worth more than the current
grade. It is the difference between what the garment is and what it will be.

## What it does not tell you

**Authenticity.** Same as everywhere else in the product: condition grading is
not authentication.

**That the tag belongs to this garment.** A passport resolves the code to a
record. It cannot see what you are holding. If a tag is loose or looks
transferred, treat it as unverified, and compare the photos on the passport
against the garment in your hands.

That comparison is the check worth doing in a shop: passport photos of a navy
coat and a black coat in front of you is a conversation to have with the seller.

## When it does not resolve

**Not found.** The code does not exist, or was mistyped.

**Not published.** The garment has a passport but the owner has not made it
public. Not a red flag on its own; simply nothing to see.

**Withheld.** Something on it is under moderation. Treat it as unverified until
it clears.

## Getting one for your own clothes

Sellers create passports on graded items and print the tags. Buyers who receive
one can claim it, so the record follows the garment into your ownership and
carries on if you sell it later.

That is the long game of the whole feature: a garment with five years of honest
condition history is worth more at resale than an identical garment with none,
and the person who benefits from starting it is whoever holds it now.

## Using it in a shop

The practical version, at a rail with one hand free.

Scan the tag. Look at two things: the most recent grade, and whether the photos
on the passport look like the garment you are holding. Everything else can wait
until you are sitting down.

Those two checks take about fifteen seconds and catch the two failures that
matter: a garment considerably worse than its record, and a tag that has come off
something else.

## For sellers at markets

A printed tag with a QR code turns a conversation into a demonstration.

"It's in great condition" is what every stall says. A code somebody can scan
standing in front of you, showing a dated assessment with the photographs it was
made from, is a different kind of claim, and it is the reason the scan page needs
no account: a verification a buyer cannot perform is not a verification.

## Privacy

A passport shows what its owner published. Ownership history appears only where
the previous owners chose to record it, and nothing about a private garment
becomes public because it was graded.
