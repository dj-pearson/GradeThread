Work through this in order. Most surprising grades resolve at step one or two,
and disputing first costs you a day to learn something a minute would have told
you.

## 1. Read the confidence figure

**Low confidence** means the assessment is standing on thin evidence. The number
may still be about right, but a reviewer looking at the same photographs will
reach much the same conclusion, so disputing it is slow and better photos are
fast.

**High confidence** with a grade you disagree with is the case worth disputing.
The assessment was sure, and if it is wrong that is genuinely useful to know.

## 2. Read the factors, not the total

The overall is a weighted average, and it hides which part disagreed with you.

A jumper you think of as an 8 that came back 6.8 has usually lost its points in
one place. If that place is fabric, look at the surface in the photo at full
zoom: pilling is far more visible to a camera than to somebody who has owned the
thing for two years.

Fabric is 30 percent, so half a point there moves the total more than a full
point on odour does.

## 3. Read the written summary

It usually names what it found. If it mentions a flaw you cannot see, it is
inferring from something adjacent, and that inference is generally why confidence
came out lower than you expected.

Photograph that area properly and resubmit. This resolves more cases than any
other single action.

## 4. Look at your photos as evidence

The three questions worth asking:

**Is the care label legible?** If not, the fabric composition is unknown, and
composition changes what counts as wear. That is a direct hit on the heaviest
factor.

**Is the back actually photographed?** A front-only set systematically over-reads
condition until it does not, and the back is where collar, seat and shoulder wear
lives.

**Is anything folded?** A fold hides a strip of the garment, and the assessment
is appropriately cautious about what it cannot see.

## 5. Check your own calibration

Grade something you would confidently call a 9. A near-new item with tags, in
good light, photographed properly.

If that comes back around 9, the scale is reading correctly and the surprise is
about the specific garments. If it comes back at 7, something systematic is going
on and that is worth reporting with the submission.

This step catches the commonest expectation problem: what people call excellent
in a listing is often a 7, because listings have been graded generously for
twenty years and the eye adjusts.

## 6. Then dispute it

If the confidence was high, the factors do not explain it, and your calibration
check read correctly, dispute it. A human reviewer looks at the same photographs.

Say which factor you disagree with and why. "This is a 7 not a 5" is harder to
act on than "the fabric score reads that texture as pilling and it is the weave".

See [Disputes and regrades](/help/grading/disputes-and-regrades).

## If the garment changed

Cleaned, repaired, or the missing button found: that is a regrade rather than a
dispute. The original grade was right about the garment it saw.

## Where expectations usually drift

Two decades of listings describing everything as excellent have moved most
people's internal scale upwards.

The commonest surprise is a garment somebody would list as "excellent" coming
back around 7, which is Very Good: light, even wear that does not affect look or
function. That is an accurate description of most well-kept second-hand clothing,
and it is a lower number than the word people would have used.

Reading [The grading scale](/help/grading/the-grading-scale) once, with a garment
in your hand, resets that faster than any amount of arguing with individual
reports.

## What to send if you report one

The submission itself, which factor you disagree with, and what you would have
scored it.

That last one sounds presumptuous and is genuinely useful: the gap between your
number and ours is the thing being investigated, and it cannot be worked out from
a complaint that the grade is too low.
