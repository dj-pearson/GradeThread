The Android app does the same job as the iPhone one, for the same reason:
photographing a garment is a phone task, and moving photos to a computer before
uploading them is the step at which people give up.

## Signing in

Sign in with Google is the smoothest route on Android. Email and password works
too.

Either reaches the same account you use on the web, so anything you graded on a
desktop is already there and anything you grade here appears there.

## What it does

**Capture.** Guided photo capture with the required set as prompts, so you are
not trying to remember whether you took the care label.

**Submit and read.** Send for grading and get the report on the same device.

**Your submissions.** Everything graded, with certificates.

**Share into it.** Send photos from the gallery or any other app through
Android's share sheet.

**Notifications.** A push when a grade lands.

## Where it differs from iOS

The two apps are built separately, natively for each platform, rather than one
codebase pretending to be both. That is why they feel like their platforms
rather than like each other.

Practically, the differences you will notice are layout conventions, how the
share sheet behaves, and where settings live. Nothing in the workflow differs.

## What belongs on a desktop

The same split as on iPhone. Photograph and grade on the phone; write listings,
run the AutoLister and reconcile payouts at a desk.

FlipDesk's dense screens, particularly anything showing many items at once, are
the wrong shape for a phone and will stay that way.

## Offline

Photos taken without a connection are held and uploaded when one returns.
Grading needs the network, because the assessment runs on a server.

Useful at a car boot sale or a fair with poor signal: capture everything there,
let it upload on the way back.

## Permissions

The app asks for the camera, for photo access, and for notifications.

The camera is the point. Photo access is for choosing pictures you already took
rather than shooting new ones. Notifications are how you learn a grade landed
without checking, and declining them costs you only that.

Each is asked for when it is first needed rather than all at once on first
launch, so it is clear which feature is asking.

## Getting it

From Google Play. Same account as the web and as the iPhone app, so there is
nothing to set up twice.

## Notifications

Allow them if you grade regularly. A push when a report lands is the difference
between getting on with something else and opening the app every ninety seconds
to check.

The notification says what the item was and what it scored, so it answers the
question rather than prompting you to go and ask it.

Declining costs you only that, and Android's own settings let you change your
mind later.

## Storage and photos

Photos you take through the app are held until they upload, then cleared. The app
does not accumulate a second copy of your camera roll.

If you are working somewhere with no signal, expect the pending queue to grow
until you have a connection. That is the intended behaviour, and it is what lets
you photograph forty things at a fair and deal with them later.

## When to use the web instead

Anything with many items on screen at once: the FlipDesk tables, the AutoLister
queue, reconciliation, the composer.

That is a deliberate split rather than a gap. Those screens need width, and a
phone-shaped forty-row table helps nobody. Photograph on the phone, list at a
desk, same data on both.
