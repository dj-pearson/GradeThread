A trust score answers one question: when this seller ships a graded item, does it
arrive as graded. Not whether they were pleasant, not whether postage was fast.
Those are the marketplace's job.

## What it is built from

**Confirmed outcomes.** A buyer received a graded item and said whether it
matched its certificate.

That confirmation is the load-bearing part. An opinion is cheap and a confirmed
comparison against a dated, public assessment is not, which is why the score
weights the second and ignores the first.

**Volume and consistency.** Ten confirmed matches say more than one, and a
consistent record says more than an erratic one with the same average.

**Time.** Recent evidence counts for more than old, because a seller's habits
change.

## What it deliberately refuses to measure

**Unconfirmed impressions.** A buyer who did not confirm has not provided
evidence, and treating silence as approval or as complaint would both be wrong.

**A single bad experience.** One dispute against forty confirmed matches is
noise, and a system that let one loud outcome dominate would be a system worth
gaming by anybody with a grudge.

**Anything from the browser extension's seller memory.** That feature records
your own reads of a seller on your own device, and it is deliberately never sent
anywhere or written to any reputation record. A handful of unconfirmed reads from
listing photos is not a basis for scoring somebody's honesty in public, and
[Seller memory](/help/extension/seller-memory) explains why at more length.

## Why it cannot be bought

There is no purchase that improves it, no promotion that adjusts it, and no
appeal that raises it without evidence.

That is not strictness for its own sake. A trust signal that can be bought is a
trust signal that tells a buyer nothing, and it would take one instance of that
becoming known to make the entire number worthless.

## For sellers

The way to improve it is to grade honestly and ship what the certificate says.

Two habits do most of the work. Photograph flaws rather than hiding them, since a
disclosed flaw is in the certificate and cannot be a discrepancy. And regrade
after cleaning or repairing, so the certificate describes the garment you are
actually sending.

Both of those raise your grades as well as your standing, which is the incentive
working the right way round.

## For buyers

Read it as one signal among several, and read what it is made of rather than only
the number.

A new seller with a short record is not a bad seller, they are an unknown one. A
seller with a long record of confirmed matches is a genuinely different
proposition, and it is the only signal in resale that is built from checkable
outcomes rather than from how a message was phrased.

## The verified directory

Sellers who opt in appear in [the verified directory](/verified), ranked by
graded volume and average condition grade. It is public, opt-in, and the same
data as the profile.

## Reading a short record fairly

A new seller has no record, and that is not the same as a bad one.

The honest reading of a thin record is "unknown", and the sensible response is to
lean on the other signals: the certificate itself, the photographs it was made
from, and the marketplace's own protections. All three work regardless of
standing.

A seller with fifty confirmed matches has told you something no amount of
messaging could. A seller with three has told you very little, in either
direction.

## Why confirmations are worth earning

The score exists because buyers confirm outcomes, and buyers confirm outcomes
because it takes ten seconds and earns credits.

That loop is the whole mechanism. Without confirmations there is no evidence,
without evidence there is no score, and without a score the only trust signal in
resale is once again how nicely somebody writes a listing.
