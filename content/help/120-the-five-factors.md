The overall grade is a weighted average of five factors. This is what each one
looks at, what drags it down, and what the weighting is actually telling you.

## The weights

| Factor | Weight |
|---|---|
| Fabric condition | 30% |
| Structural integrity | 25% |
| Cosmetic appearance | 20% |
| Functional elements | 15% |
| Odour and cleanliness | 10% |

Each factor is scored in half-point steps from 1.0 to 10.0. The overall is the
weighted average, rounded to one decimal.

These weights are fixed and published, and that is the whole reason the scale
is worth anything. A rubric you can reweight is a rubric you can argue into any
answer you wanted.

## Fabric condition, 30 percent

The material itself. Pilling, thinning at stress points, fading, stretched-out
ribbing, holes, snags, felting on wool, the cracked print on a tee.

It carries the most weight because it is the least fixable thing on a garment.
A buyer can sew a button back on. Nobody unpills a jumper back to new. Fabric
condition is, in practice, how much life the garment has left.

This is also why the care label photo matters. Composition changes what counts
as wear: pilling on lambswool at eighteen months is normal, and the same
pilling on a synthetic blend is not.

## Structural integrity, 25 percent

How the garment is put together and whether it still holds. Seams, hems,
linings, shoulder shape, whether a jacket still hangs the way it was cut.

Split seams and dropped hems live here. So does the thing sellers most often
miss: a garment that has lost its shape. A blazer with a collapsed shoulder
line is structurally worse than one with a small repaired seam, even though the
seam is the visible flaw.

## Cosmetic appearance, 20 percent

Marks. Stains, discolouration, yellowing at the underarm or the collar,
transfer from a bag strap, sun fade on one side.

Cosmetic sits third because much of it comes out. A fresh mark is often a wash
away, and the grade reflects the garment as photographed, not as it might be
after cleaning. If you clean it, regrade it; the difference is frequently worth
more than the effort.

## Functional elements, 15 percent

The hardware and the parts that do a job. Zips, buttons, poppers, hooks and
eyes, drawstrings, elastic, velcro.

A broken zip is annoying and cheap. Fifteen percent is roughly the honest price
of that annoyance: enough that it should be disclosed, not so much that it
tanks an otherwise excellent coat.

Missing spare buttons do not count against you. A missing functional button
does.

## Odour and cleanliness, 10 percent

Smoke, damp, mustiness from long storage, pet smell, perfume that has soaked in.

Lowest weight, because most of it launders out, and because it is the factor
photographs are worst at showing. The model infers it from context: visible
soiling, storage marks, the kind of yellowing that comes with smoke exposure.

If a garment smells and looks clean, the grade will not catch it and you should
say so in the listing anyway. A returned item costs more than the honesty did.

## What the weighting means for you

Two practical consequences.

**Repair fabric first.** If you are deciding what to fix before listing, a
half-point of fabric is worth double a half-point of function. Depilling a
jumper moves the number more than replacing a button does.

**Do not hide the small stuff.** A photographed flaw costs a fraction of a
point in the factor it belongs to. An unphotographed flaw costs you confidence,
which sends the grade to human review, and then costs you a return when the
buyer finds it. See
[Confidence and human review](/help/grading/confidence-and-human-review).
