Condition grading only works if the scale means the same thing every time. This
is the scale, what anchors each point on it, and why the number matters more
than the tier name it comes with.

## The scale

Grades run from 1.0 to 10.0, reported to one decimal place. 8.4 and 8.5 are
different grades and the difference is real.

Seven named tiers anchor it:

| Tier | Anchors | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| NWT | 10 | New with tags. Unworn, original retail tags attached. |
| NWOT | 9 | New without tags. Unworn, tags removed. |
| Excellent | 8 | Gently used, no notable flaws, looks nearly new. |
| Very Good | 7 | Light, even wear that does not affect look or function. |
| Good | 6 | Visible wear, entirely wearable. |
| Fair | 5 | Clear flaws, still usable. |
| Poor | 3 to 4 | Damaged, or sold for parts and repair. |

Below 3 is a garment that is not really being sold as clothing.

## Why the tiers anchor rather than span

Each tier names a point, not a range. Excellent anchors 8, which means an 8.0
is squarely Excellent and a 7.6 and an 8.3 both sit near it from either side.

That is deliberate. A scale where "Excellent" means anything from 7.5 to 8.4
invites the same argument the old vocabulary did, about which side of a line
something falls. Anchoring instead says: this is what an 8 looks like, and your
garment is a bit above or a bit below.

## Where the number comes from

The overall grade is a weighted average of five factors, each scored in
half-point steps. The weights are fixed and published:

- Fabric condition, 30 percent
- Structural integrity, 25 percent
- Cosmetic appearance, 20 percent
- Functional elements, 15 percent
- Odour and cleanliness, 10 percent

[The five factors](/help/grading/the-five-factors) goes through what each one
covers and what pushes it down.

The half-point steps are why factor scores look coarse and the overall does
not. A factor is a 7.5 or an 8.0, never a 7.7. The weighting produces the
decimal.

## What the numbers are actually for

Two uses, and they are different.

**Comparison.** Two jackets described as Very Good are not the same jacket. A
7.4 and a 6.6 are visibly different garments, and the buyer deciding between
them has, for the first time, something to decide with.

**Pricing.** Condition is one of the largest levers on resale price, and it
moves smoothly rather than in seven steps. The
[Condition Index](/condition-index) shows what the curve looks like for real
sold comps, and the shape of it is the argument for grading at all.

## How it lines up with marketplaces

Every platform has its own condition dropdown and none of them agree with each
other. eBay's "Pre-owned" spans a 9 down to a 3. Poshmark's tiers are seller
judgement with no published definition.

The scale does not replace those; you still pick the platform's value, and
FlipDesk suggests the right one from the grade. What it adds is a number
underneath, with a certificate a sceptical buyer can open.

[Marketplace condition standards](/grading/platform-standards) covers each
platform's own scheme and how a grade maps onto it.

## Printing it

There is a free printable version of this table at
[the condition chart](/grading/condition-chart). Sellers who source in person
tend to keep one in the car, because deciding whether something is a 6 or an 8
at the rail is the decision that determines whether buying it was worth it.
