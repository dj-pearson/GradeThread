Two photo sets, two jobs. FlipDesk keeps them separate on purpose, and mixing
them up is the commonest reason a listing underperforms or a grade comes back
cautious.

## The difference

**Grading photos** are flat, evenly lit, shot straight down, and boring. They
exist so the condition can be assessed accurately, and every stylistic choice
that makes a photo attractive makes it worse at that job.

**Listing photos** are styled to sell. On a hanger or a model, at a flattering
angle, warm, cropped so the thumbnail reads at the size a phone shows it.

Use the wrong set and you get either a listing that looks like an evidence
photograph or a grade held for human review because the garment was on a hanger
at an angle in warm light.

## The shots that sell

**Front, full.** The thumbnail. This is the one photo most buyers ever look at,
so it is worth reshooting until it is right. Fill the frame, get the colour
honest, no clutter.

**Back, full.** Buyers assume the worst about anything they cannot see.

**The label.** Brand and size, legible. It is the fastest proof that the item is
what the title says.

**Details.** Fabric texture, hardware, a pattern, a cuff. These are what somebody
zooms into.

**Flaws.** Every one you would mention in the description. Photographing a flaw
is what stops it becoming a return, and a buyer who saw the mark before buying
does not open a case about it.

**Flat lay or on-body.** One shot showing how it hangs. A garment photographed
only flat is hard to judge as clothing.

## The required set

FlipDesk checks for a required set before it lets you publish, and it tells you
which type is missing rather than blocking with a generic error.

The types it recognises are front, back, tag, detail, defect and flatlay. Which
of them are required depends on what you are listing, and a defect shot is
required when the item has a recorded flaw, which is the check that most often
catches something.

The point is not bureaucratic. Every one of those types corresponds to a
question a buyer asks, and a listing missing one is a listing that generates
messages instead of sales.

## Order matters

The first photo is the thumbnail on every channel, and the thumbnail decides
whether anybody sees the other twelve.

Reorder by dragging. Put the strongest full-front shot first, the back second,
and the label or the best detail third. Flaws belong in the set but not at the
front.

## Editing

Crop and rotate in FlipDesk rather than in a separate app, because the edited
version is what publishes and a separate app means two copies that can disagree.

Straighten anything crooked. Crop out the floor. Do not adjust colour past
honesty: a jumper that photographs warmer than it is will be returned by
somebody who ordered navy and got teal, and no amount of saturation makes that
a good trade.

## Reusing across channels

Photos attach to the item, not to the listing, so cross-posting the same item
carries the same set to every channel automatically.

Channels differ in how many they accept and in aspect ratio preference. FlipDesk
sends as many as each will take, in your order, so the thumbnail is the same
everywhere and a buyer who sees your listing twice sees the same garment.

## Reshoot the first photo

If one thing in this article is worth acting on, it is that the first photo
carries the listing.

It is the thumbnail in search results, the image in a saved-search email, and
the picture somebody scrolls past. The other twelve photos only matter to
somebody the first one already stopped.

Sellers routinely spend twenty minutes on a description nobody reads and thirty
seconds on the photo that decides whether the description is ever opened.
