A grade is one number between 1.0 and 10.0 describing the physical condition of
one garment. It is not a price, not a rating of the brand, and not a verdict on
whether the item is genuine. This article is about what it does say.

## The number

The overall grade is rounded to one decimal place, so 8.4 and 8.5 are different
grades and both are meaningful.

It is a weighted average of five factors, each scored in half-point steps:

| Factor | Weight | What it covers |
|---|---|---|
| Fabric condition | 30% | Pilling, thinning, fading, stretch, holes |
| Structural integrity | 25% | Seams, hems, shape, linings |
| Cosmetic appearance | 20% | Stains, marks, discolouration |
| Functional elements | 15% | Zips, buttons, hooks, elastic |
| Odour and cleanliness | 10% | Smoke, damp, pet, storage |

Those weights are fixed and public. They are the reason the scale means
anything: two people grading the same jacket cannot weight fabric differently
to reach the answer they wanted.

The weighting also tells you where the money is. A garment with perfect fabric
and a broken zip scores far higher than one with a working zip and a pilled
surface, because fabric is worth double what function is. If you are deciding
what to repair before listing, repair the fabric.

## The tiers

Every grade also carries a tier name, because that is what a buyer skims:

- **NWT** anchors 10. Brand new, unworn, original tags attached.
- **NWOT** anchors 9. New and unworn, tags gone.
- **Excellent** anchors 8. Gently used, no notable flaws, looks nearly new.
- **Very Good** anchors 7. Light, even wear that does not affect look or use.
- **Good** anchors 6. Visible wear, fully wearable.
- **Fair** anchors 5. Clear flaws, still usable.
- **Poor** anchors 3 to 4. Damaged, or for parts and repair.

The tier is the headline and the number is the comparison. Two jackets both
described as Very Good can be a 7.4 and a 6.6, and the buyer choosing between
them can now see that.

## How it maps to a marketplace

Every marketplace has its own condition dropdown and none of them agree. eBay's
"Pre-owned" covers everything from a 9 to a 3. Poshmark's "Excellent Used
Condition" is a seller's own judgement with no definition behind it.

A GradeThread grade sits underneath those and gives them a number. You still
pick the marketplace's own condition value, and FlipDesk suggests the right one
from the grade, but the certificate is what a sceptical buyer clicks.
[Marketplace condition standards](/grading/platform-standards) covers each
platform's own scheme.

## What it does not claim

A grade is a condition assessment produced by an automated system from your
photos. Three things it is explicitly not:

**Not an authenticity check.** The model does not decide whether a bag is real.
Condition grading and authentication are different services with different
methods. See
[What grading does not check](/help/grading/what-grading-does-not-check).

**Not a valuation.** Condition is one of the biggest levers on resale price, but
brand, size, season and demand move it more. The Condition Index shows how
price moves with grade, and that is a different tool.

**Not a guarantee against every flaw.** It assesses what the photos show. A
stain on the inside of a sleeve that nobody photographed is not in the grade,
which is why hiding a flaw is a bad strategy rather than a clever one.

## Why a fixed rubric matters

The reason a standardized grade is worth anything to a buyer is that it cannot
be flattered. A seller writing their own description grades generously without
meaning to. A published rubric with fixed weights, applied the same way every
time, is the thing that makes "8.2" a fact instead of an adjective.

That is also why the methodology is public rather than a black box. You can
read [how the model is trained and evaluated](/grading/methodology) and check
the accuracy figures for yourself.
