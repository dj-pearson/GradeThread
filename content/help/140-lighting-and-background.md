Two photos of the same jacket, one by a window and one under a kitchen light,
can grade differently. That is not the model being unreliable; it is the two
photos genuinely containing different information. This is how to make sure the
photo contains the garment rather than the room.

## Light

**Best: an overcast day, near a window, indoors.** Cloud is a diffuser the size
of the sky. It produces even light with soft shadows, which is exactly what a
lightbox is engineered to fake.

**Avoid: direct sunlight.** It creates hard-edged shadows, and a hard shadow
across a sleeve reads as a mark. It also blows out highlights, so a white shirt
loses the texture that fabric condition is scored from.

**Avoid: overhead room lights alone.** A ceiling bulb directly above a flat
garment produces a hotspot in the middle and falloff at the edges, so the hem
looks darker and more worn than it is.

**Avoid: mixing sources.** A window plus a lamp gives you two colour
temperatures in one frame, and the garment ends up warm on one side and cool on
the other. Turn the lamp off.

## Shooting at night

Sometimes the only time you have is after dark. It is workable.

Use one light, not several. Point it at a white wall or the ceiling and let the
bounce light the garment, rather than aiming it straight down. Bounced light is
soft; direct light is not.

If your phone lets you set white balance, set it. If not, take one photo of
something you know is white in the same light, which gives you a reference to
check against later.

Do not use the flash. On-camera flash flattens texture completely, and texture
is what fabric condition is read from.

## Background

Plain, matte, and a different tone from the garment.

A bare wooden floor, a plain table, a single-colour sheet pulled flat. What you
are avoiding is anything that puts pattern or texture into the frame that is
not on the garment: a patterned duvet, a rug, a wood grain so strong it
competes.

Contrast matters too. A cream jumper on a cream sheet loses its edges, and the
shape of the garment is part of what structural integrity is scored from. Put a
light garment on a darker ground and a dark one on a lighter ground.

## The garment itself

Flat, smoothed, and fully in frame.

Smooth out the folds. Every fold is a strip of the garment the model cannot
assess, and it will be appropriately cautious about the part it cannot see,
which means a lower confidence rather than a lower grade.

Fill the frame. A garment occupying a third of the picture is a garment at a
third of the resolution, and pilling is a small-detail judgement.

Shoot straight down. An angled shot foreshortens one end, distorts the shape,
and makes a straight hem look uneven.

## What this actually buys you

Two things, and only one of them is the grade.

The obvious one is accuracy. Better photos mean the score matches what you
would say yourself, which is when you start trusting it.

The less obvious one is speed. A grade below the confidence threshold is held
for a human reviewer instead of published, and that takes longer than a minute.
Most held grades are held because of the photos, not because the garment was
genuinely ambiguous. Two minutes of setup is the difference between a report
now and a report later.

## The listing photos are a different job

Do not reuse these for your listing, and do not use listing photos for grading.

A listing photo is styled to sell: on a model or a hanger, at an angle, warmly
lit, cropped for a thumbnail. A grading photo is flat, even and deliberately
boring. Trying to do both with one set gives you a mediocre listing and a
cautious grade.
